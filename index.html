Externship List

A search-and-rank tool that helps UVA architecture students choose their top 15 externships from 64 firm postings.

RoleSolo: UX, visual design, build
ForUVA School of Architecture students
ToolsHTML, CSS, JavaScript, WebGL, GitHub Pages
StatusIn review with the Career Development Office
The problem

64 postings in one long, small-text document students couldn't download, and they had to rank 15.

What I made

A searchable list with filters, notes on each firm, and a drag-to-rank shortlist.

Where it is

Live on GitHub Pages and in review with the Career Development Office before release.

Problem & users

Why a document wasn't enough, and what students needed instead+

Every year the School of Architecture shares January externships with students. This year there were 64 postings in one long, small-text document that couldn't be downloaded. To choose and rank their top 15, students had to scroll back and forth comparing pay, dates, location, format and which programs each firm would take.

I work in the school's Career Development Office and I'm a student choosing externships myself, so I knew both what the postings contained and how hard they were to compare.

What students needed

  • Rule firms out fast. Undergrads and grad students are eligible for different firms, and some programs only have a handful of options.
  • Compare at a glance. Pay, dates, in-person or hybrid, and paid vs. shadowing-only are the facts you check first.
  • Keep track of their thinking. Notes and tags that stay with each firm.
  • End with a ranked list. The real goal is an ordered top 15, not a pile of bookmarks.

Structuring the data

Turning 64 postings into fields students can filter by+

Before designing any screens, I went through all 64 postings and pulled out the fields they shared: firm, location, dates, hourly rate, opportunity type, format, and eligibility by program. Each posting's full description stays word for word, so students can trust that nothing was lost or reworded.

Once the postings were structured, the filters followed from how students narrow down: by program first, then pay type, dates, format and state.

The program filter shows how many firms each program can apply to (47 for Architecture, 5 for the PhD), so students see right away how much room they have to be picky.

Design decisions

Six choices about what to show, what to hide, and what to protect+

Lead with the decision

Cards show location, dates, format, opportunity type and rate. The full posting waits in a detail panel, so the list stays easy to scan.

Make paid vs. shadowing obvious

Paid externships get a solid tag and shadowing-only ones get a dashed outline, so the difference reads at a glance, even without color.

Your notes live with the firm

Each firm has a star, your own tags and a notes field. "My tags" and "Starred only" filters bring your shortlist back later.

No account, nothing shared

Everything a student adds saves only in their own browser. There's no sign-up, and nobody else can see which firms they picked.

Be clear about what this is

A notice at the top says the tool is unofficial and student-made, and to confirm details on the official list before applying.

Same tool on any screen

A list with a side panel on desktop, cards and bottom sheets on a phone, plus reduced-motion support for the animated background.

The ranking step

From starred firms to an ordered top 15+

Starring a firm adds it to My list. There, students drag firms into order, and a counter shows how close they are to the target of 15.

When they're done, Copy list copies the ranked list so they can paste it wherever they submit their choices.

This was the step the original document had no answer for. Reading postings was possible; comparing them and committing to an order was the hard part.

Build & status

How it's built and where it stands+

It's one lightweight page hosted on GitHub Pages, so it loads quickly on a phone and the office can keep it running with nothing to install or maintain. It's now with the Career Development Office for review before it goes out to students.

DinkMatch

A mobile web app that helps pickleball players fill a booked court with partners at their skill level.

RoleSolo: research, UX, visual design, build
Tested with7 players, 5 rounds of feedback
ToolsHTML, CSS, JavaScript, Clerk, Vercel, Cursor
StatusWorking MVP with demo data
The problem

Players could book a court at my gym but had no easy way to find three partners at their level.

What I made

A working app to find, create, join and score doubles games, filtered by skill.

How I tested it

5 rounds of feedback with 7 players, changing the design after each round.

Problem & research

The moment no existing app was built for+

At my gym, Lifetime Fitness, booking a court was easy. Filling it was not. Finding partners meant group chats and guesswork, and games often ended up lopsided because skill levels didn't match. For players who track a DUPR rating, a mismatched game can also hurt their score.

Before designing, I researched how existing apps handle courts, ratings and scheduling. None of them focused on the moment I cared about: you have a court and a time, and you need three more people who play at your level.

Scope & key screens

One core loop: find a game, join it, play, score it+

I scoped the first version to that loop and deliberately left out a backend, chat and leagues, so I could get something real in front of players quickly. The app runs on seeded demo data, and anyone can try it with "Continue with demo."

  • Home: your rating, reliability and upcoming games.
  • Open plays: games you can join, filtered by date, type and skill.
  • Create: post a game for a court you've booked.
  • Match page: who's in, which spots are open, and a link to share.
  • You: your profile, stats and past games.

Design decisions

Making a four-person game easy to read and easy to trust+

Open spots as empty seats

Each game shows four seats, so you can see at a glance how many players are still needed.

Smart defaults

A new game starts as today, DUPR-rated, with a skill range of ±0.1 around your rating, so most games take a few taps to post.

Trust signals

Every player shows their rating, reliability and show rate, because no-shows and inflated self-ratings came up again and again in my research.

Log any game

Players can record games they played somewhere else, not only games set up in the app.

Testing with players

5 rounds of feedback with 7 players at my gym+

I put each version in front of players at my gym, found where they hit friction, and changed the design before the next round.

  • Simpler navigation. The app now has four tabs: Home, Open plays, Create and You.
  • Autofill. Forms start from your rating and the most common choices, so players don't retype the same details for every game.

What's next

A shared backend and an easier skill control+
  • A shared backend so games sync between players instead of living on one device.
  • An easier skill-range control. Research on players over 40 showed that two-handle sliders are hard to use on a touchscreen, so I want to replace the slider with something you can set with taps.
